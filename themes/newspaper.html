Front page · The Lead

Researchers say AI plateau may be a turning point, not an ending

Six prominent voices in the field agree the era of brute-force scaling has ended. Where the next surprises come from — and how soon — is the only thing they cannot agree on.

By Jihye Min & Hannah Ryu  ·  Reporting from Seoul, Palo Alto, and London  ·  11 minutes

The slow, careful conversation that researchers have been having behind closed doors broke into the open this spring. After two years of training-loss curves that flattened earlier than anyone wanted, the question that animates every conference hallway is no longer how big can we go but where do we go now.

It is a question with at least six different answers. Yann LeCun, a longtime skeptic of the autoregressive paradigm, argues that the field has been climbing a tall hill for the wrong reason. World models — systems that predict in latent space rather than tokens — are, in his view, the only road that does not end in a wall.

That is not the consensus. Andrej Karpathy, the former Tesla and OpenAI engineer who has become an evangelist for a new generation of programmers, calls today's models a new kind of computer, "Software 3.0," in which natural language is the source code. He has founded a school to teach the discipline that comes with that change. The bottleneck, in his telling, has moved from implementation to taste.

Geoffrey Hinton, who left Google in 2023 and Toronto for the lecture circuit, takes a darker view. The structural advantages of digital intelligence — perfect copy, instant transmission of weights — are not, to him, an aesthetic preference. They are a one-way ratchet. Demis Hassabis at DeepMind keeps insisting that the test of a model is what other problems it solves; protein folding, then materials, then medicine.

The youngest argument in the room belongs to Fei-Fei Li, who has wagered her new lab on spatial intelligence — the claim that text alone is too thin a substrate for genuine understanding. And then there is Ilya Sutskever, the most influential silence in the field, who said only that the era we knew is over and another, unspecified one will follow.

Together, the six map out not a single road but a coordinate plane. Continued on page A6.
