In defense of the page that loads slowly

Sometime around 2018, the web decided that everything had to be instant. We forgot to ask why. A field report from inside the small but stubborn movement to let pages take their time.

Words   Hyewon Park    Photographs   Jin Lee Reading time · 28 minutes
AboveThe light through a single south-facing window in a Berlin atelier where the magazine was edited over six weekends. Photograph by Jin Lee for The Slow Issue, March 2026.

The argument starts in a kitchen. A friend, a software engineer who I will not name because he might be embarrassed, has just spent forty minutes optimizing a page from 1.2 seconds to 380 milliseconds. He asks me, with the genuine curiosity of someone who has not had this thought before, whether anyone has actually noticed.

I think about this for a long time. I do not have a good answer.

The answer that we are supposed to give is yes — that each hundred milliseconds counts, that conversion rates degrade at the rate of x percent per second, that users leave. There are studies. There are decks with these studies in them. But the studies are not, in fact, what motivates most of the optimization that I see in the wild. What motivates it is a kind of quiet shame: a sense that anything below 60 frames a second is a moral failure, that the existence of a perceptible delay is a confession of laziness.

This is, I will argue, the wrong frame. The web has always known how to be fast. It also has always known how to be other things. Stillness, for example. Patience. Anticipation. The slow page is not a failure of the fast page; it is a different page with a different relationship to the reader's body.

A page that loads slowly is doing two things at once: arriving, and asking you to be present for the arrival. — from a conversation with Min-jung, May 2025

Consider the small movement of writers, designers, and gardeners (yes, gardeners) who have been quietly building an alternative web in the last three years. Naive Yearly, a magazine that exists only as one PDF per year, takes nine seconds to render its hero illustration on a fast connection. The renderer is intentionally pessimal. The illustration is, in fact, the experience.

Or take The Quiet Index, the small searchable database of spaces in the city where it is permitted to do nothing. The index loads each entry as a separate fade, one every 600 milliseconds. There is a button to skip the animation; it is grey, and it lives at the bottom of the page, and almost nobody clicks it.

What is happening on these pages is not a failure of optimization. It is the careful design of a tempo. Tempo is, as any pianist will tell you, the part of music that the audience hears without hearing. It is the floor that the rest of the music stands on. A web page also has a tempo, whether or not the people who built it noticed.

I have spent the last six months interviewing the people who deliberately chose slower tempos for their work. None of them used the word "slow" in the way that you and I would. The word that came up, again and again, was given. The page is given to you at the rate at which it can be received.

This is not a manifesto. It is not even an argument with the engineers; the engineers are right that fast is, in many cases, what their pages are for. But there is a category of page — the page that wants to be present, not transactional — that has been quietly subjugated to the metric of fast, and it is in those cases that I want to enter a small, polite plea on behalf of tempo.

The glossary, partial

Tempo
The rate at which a page is willing to be received. Distinct from speed; a page can be quick and still arrhythmic.
Givenness
A property of slow pages: they arrive at a rate the reader cannot accelerate, and so the reader becomes present for the arrival.
Pessimal renderer
A renderer designed to be slower than necessary, in service of an aesthetic or attentional goal. See Naive Yearly.
Soft optimization
Making the user's relationship with the page faster, even when the page itself is not. The companion to hard optimization.
